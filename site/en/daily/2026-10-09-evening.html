Explainer

How do you tell short covering apart from value buying? Both can drive a sharp bounce, like today's, but they work through different mechanisms. Short covering is mechanical: traders who bet on falling prices by going short are forced to buy back those same contracts to limit their losses once price moves against them — that buying triggers more buying, a "bear squeeze." It's fast but often shallow, and tends to fade within a session or two. Value buying is slower: investors genuinely buy stocks they see as priced below fair value, betting the price will converge back up. The most reliable way to tell them apart is to watch for confirmation: a pure short-covering rally tends to stall near resistance and fade the next day, while a rally with real value buying behind it keeps going, open interest in long positions rises, and India VIX falls even as prices climb. Today's move — IT joined by FMCG and, at midday, the broader market — looks more broad-based than a single-sector short squeeze, but the real confirmation will only come from Monday's follow-through.

Today's Concept
Averaging Down vs. Pyramiding

Averaging down means buying more of a losing position to lower your average cost — risky, since you're adding to a trade that's already going against you. Pyramiding is the opposite: adding to a winning position as it moves in your favor. More experienced traders favor pyramiding into strength over averaging into weakness, since it means the market is confirming the thesis rather than you fighting it. This whole week's arc — Thursday's crash followed by today's sharp bounce — is exactly the scenario where this distinction matters: averaging down into yesterday's falling knife, and pyramiding too early into today's unconfirmed bounce, both carry real risk until the trend is genuinely confirmed. This is an educational concept, not a recommendation to place the trade.
