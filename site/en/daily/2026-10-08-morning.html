Explainer

Why do rising bond yields pull stocks down, even without any "bad news"? Stocks and bonds compete for the same investor money. When Treasury yields rise (as they did Wednesday night, the 10-year toward ~5.31% and the 30-year toward ~5.68%), bonds start offering a safer, guaranteed return — making stocks, whose returns are uncertain, relatively less attractive. The second mechanism is "discounting": a company's value today equals the present value of its future earnings, and higher yields mean discounting those future earnings at a higher rate — which lowers their value today. This effect hits hardest the stocks whose value depends most on distant-future growth (tech and high-growth names) — exactly what played out Wednesday night, with both the Nasdaq and the S&P 500 closing -0.22% lower without any specific negative headline.

Strategy Corner
Today's Concept
Covered Call

If you already own a stock, sell an out-of-the-money call option against it — "covered" because owning the underlying shares covers the risk if the option gets exercised. It's an income-generating strategy: you collect the premium, but your upside is capped at the sold strike if the stock rallies past it. This fits a day like today well — after a strong rally yesterday, the market looks set for a pause-and-consolidate mode while it waits for TCS's results, where generating some extra income on existing holdings can make more sense than a pure directional bet. This is an educational concept, not a recommendation to place the trade.
