Explainer: Why IPOs Price in a Band, Not a Fixed Number

Jio Platforms opens tomorrow at a price band of ₹1,065-1,119 — not a fixed number. This is "book building," the standard way large IPOs are priced today. The company and its merchant bankers set a range — a floor price (the band's lower end) and a cap price (the upper end) — and investors then bid within that range, specifying both a price and the quantity they want, revealing their actual demand. As bidding progresses, the exchange calculates a "cut-off price" — the point where total demand equals the total shares on offer — and that becomes the final issue price, which can land anywhere within the band. This beats a fixed-price IPO because it lets real demand discover the price, rather than making the company guess what the market will pay. Retail investors can bid "at cut-off," meaning whatever the final price turns out to be, they're automatically allotted shares as long as their bid falls within the band. For a mega-IPO like Jio's, this mechanism matters even more — anchor-book demand alone of $20 billion+ can push the final price close to the cap.

Strategy Corner
Today's Concept
Iron Condor

An Iron Condor sells an out-of-the-money call spread and an out-of-the-money put spread at the same time. You collect premium upfront and keep it if the underlying stays within a range through expiry — the trade that profits from a market going nowhere. Risk is capped on both sides, which is why it's a popular income strategy in sideways markets. A zero-movement holiday session like today — where literally no price discovery happened all day — is exactly the kind of backdrop where range-bound strategies like this one get discussed, even as real volatility is expected once Wednesday's reopening tests both the bank-stock follow-through and the 22,800 support. This is an educational concept only, not a recommendation to place the trade.
