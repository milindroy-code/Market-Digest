Explainer: How IPO Allotment Actually Works — Lottery, Oversubscription And Refunds

When an IPO draws more demand than the shares on offer (like Jio Platforms' ~22x oversubscription), not every applicant can get shares — the exchange (NSE/BSE) runs a computerised, SEBI-regulated lottery known as the "basis of allotment." For retail investors this works lot by lot: if you applied for one lot and your category was oversubscribed 5x, your odds of allotment are roughly 1-in-5, whatever multiple of lots you bid for — bidding for more lots only slightly improves your odds, it doesn't guarantee an allotment. Larger QIB and NII allotments are proportional rather than a full lottery. Applicants who don't get shares have their entire blocked amount (via ASBA) automatically unblocked within a few days — no manual refund request is needed. That's why an IPO being "22x subscribed" doesn't mean you'd earn 22 times your money — it means your odds of getting an allotment at all have dropped to roughly 1-in-22: the higher the oversubscription, the lower the odds.

Today's Concept
The Iron Butterfly

A tighter cousin of the iron condor: sell an at-the-money call and put, and buy further out-of-the-money call and put as protection. It pays out most if the underlying finishes exactly at the middle strike, offering a higher potential reward than a condor for a market expected to stay very still — at the cost of a narrower profitable range. This morning's muted, slightly negative GIFT Nifty signal — a flat-to-weak open with some chance of consolidation — is exactly the kind of range-bound setup where this strategy's concept applies. This is an educational concept, not a recommendation to place the trade.
