Explainer

Why can markets move sharply even after a well-expected event? When an event like an RBI rate decision is expected weeks in advance, the market gradually "prices it in" — meaning stocks and indices already reflect that expectation before the outcome even lands. Tuesday's confident close, with Nifty sitting near its resistance zone, was itself a sign of exactly that. But being "priced in" doesn't guarantee a muted reaction — if the actual outcome matches expectations closely, the move can indeed be small (the classic "buy the rumour, sell the news"); but if the RBI surprises with a more hawkish or dovish tilt than expected — or its stance language differs from what was priced in — the move can be sharp, in either direction. That's exactly why a scheduled, binary day like today is a textbook example of implied volatility for options traders: the real uncertainty isn't the rate number itself, but the language and guidance that comes with it.

Strategy Corner
Today's Concept
Long Straddle

Buy a call and a put at the same strike and expiry. You profit if the underlying makes a big move in either direction — you're betting on volatility itself, not a direction. The cost is the combined premium of both options, which is also your maximum loss if the stock sits still. Traders reach for this ahead of binary events like a Fed decision or an earnings call, exactly the kind of setup in today's edition — a scheduled RBI rate decision where the outcome could genuinely go either way, one that's educational, not a trade-placement recommendation.
