Explainer

How does a US president rejecting a geopolitical proposal move the Indian stock market? The answer runs through crude oil's "geopolitical risk premium." The Strait of Hormuz carries a huge share of the world's seaborne oil trade — when the risk of it being closed or restricted rises (as it did after Trump's Saturday rejection), traders immediately price in an extra premium on crude, even if no actual supply has been disrupted yet. This matters doubly for India because the country imports roughly 85% of its crude oil needs — a sustained $1/barrel rise can add roughly $2-2.5 billion to India's annual import bill, pressuring both the current account deficit and the rupee. That's why an indicator like GIFT Nifty, which absorbs overnight global news, is signaling lower this morning purely on crude's move — without any India-specific bad news at all.

Strategy Corner
Today's Concept
Risk-Reward Ratio

Before entering a trade, compare what you stand to lose (distance to your stop-loss) against what you stand to gain (distance to your target). A trade with a 1:3 risk-reward ratio can still be profitable overall even if you're wrong more often than you're right — this is why professional traders talk about expectancy, not just win rate. On a headline-sensitive, crude-driven session like today's — where GIFT Nifty flipped from tepid to negative overnight — defining your risk in advance and sticking to it matters more than reacting to any single news headline.
