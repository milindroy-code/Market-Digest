Global Cues

Wall Street staged a sharp rebound on Thursday: the Dow rose 316.14 points (0.61%) to 51,778.04, the S&P 500 gained 1.14% to 7,637.76, and the Nasdaq added 1.69% to close at 26,418.30 — a clean recovery from Tuesday night's Fed-hike-triggered sell-off, when the Dow had dropped 631 points. The bounce came as the 10-year Treasury yield pulled back from its highest level since 2007, snapping an eight-day rising streak, alongside easing oil prices. Tech led the rally — Nvidia and Amazon both rose more than 2%, Microsoft gained 1.5%, and a gauge of chipmakers climbed 3%.

Brent crude fell for a second straight session, down from Tuesday's $109.2 peak to around $103.6/barrel (−2.10%), after Saudi Arabia said it would restore roughly half the capacity of its East-West pipeline — damaged in a drone attack last week — within days, and bring it to full capacity within six weeks. The rupee remains weak on broad dollar strength, hovering near ₹96/$ (this week's range: ₹94.83–96.01). Gold rebounded more than 2% on Thursday to settle around $4,360/oz, as the oil rally's momentum cooled.

Explainer

Tuesday night's hawkish Fed hike sank Wall Street — so why did the very same market snap back so sharply on Thursday, when the Fed's message hadn't changed? Because stocks don't move on the policy rate alone — bond yields matter just as much. The 10-year Treasury yield, which had climbed to its highest level since 2007, snapped an eight-day rising streak on Thursday and pulled back — and that's exactly the kind of relief growth and tech stocks (whose value leans more heavily on future earnings) need most. Easing oil prices took some pressure off inflation too. Stocks like Nvidia and Amazon rose more than 2%, and a chipmaker index climbed 3% — the biggest relief showed up precisely in the sectors most sensitive to rates. The lesson: a hike being "priced in" doesn't mean the entire path after it is locked in too — the yield curve itself can become the next day's biggest driver.

Strategy Corner
Concept of the day
Protective Put

Buy a put option on a stock you already own, as insurance. If the stock falls, the put gains value and offsets the loss; if it doesn't, you've paid a small premium for peace of mind — the same logic as car insurance you hope never to use. A week like this one — a sharp Tuesday Fed-hike sell-off followed by an equally sharp Thursday rebound — is a reminder that sometimes the smartest move is insuring a position rather than exiting it.
