Explainer

Why do FII and DII flows often move in opposite directions? Wednesday showed this in action — FIIs net sold ₹10,148.40 cr, DIIs net bought ₹11,271.70 cr, and Bank Nifty rose while Nifty fell. FIIs react quickly to global signals — US yields, crude prices, geopolitical risk — and when the rupee weakens, their currency risk rises too, often sharpening their selling. DIIs run on a different calculus: steady domestic inflows via SIPs give them capital to deploy, and the very moment FIIs sell hardest is often when valuations start looking attractive to them — meaning their buying is frequently triggered by FII selling itself, a natural counter-cyclical force. That's why DIIs are often called the market's stabiliser — when FIIs apply selling pressure, DIIs cushion it with steady, retail-backed capital, preventing a full-blown freefall. This pattern doesn't always hold — sometimes both move in the same direction — but in uncertain periods like this one, it's exactly this divergence that keeps the market supported.

Strategy Corner
Today's Concept
Iron Condor

Sell an out-of-the-money call spread and an out-of-the-money put spread at the same time. You collect premium upfront and keep it if the underlying stays within a range through expiry — the trade that profits from a market going nowhere. Risk is capped on both sides, which is why it's a popular income strategy in sideways markets. Today's setup fits the tag well — Nifty's PCR is near 0.90, max pain sits at 22,750, and the index keeps getting rejected at 22,800 resistance, all pointing to a range-bound bias. In a setup like this, traders explore strategies like the Iron Condor when they judge the probability of a big directional move to be low — an educational concept, not a recommendation to place the trade.
