"The stock market is filled with individuals who know the price of everything, but the value of nothing."

— Philip Fisher

Four sessions, four red closes — no relief anywhere. From Monday through Thursday, the pattern this week was brutally clean: every single trading day closed lower for Nifty and Sensex, touching a fresh 2026 low by Thursday. It started with the weekend's Trump-Iran rejection of a Hormuz-reopening proposal (crude spiked straight to ~$106-108), then Wednesday brought Nifty's first test of its 200-week moving average (~22,600) since the Covid crash, and Thursday a different trigger entirely — US bond yields — rattled the market: the 10-year Treasury yield climbed to ~5.30% and the 30-year hit a 24-year high (~5.64%), even as Tuesday night's core PCE inflation print (the Fed's preferred gauge) actually came in cooler than expected. The result: Nifty closed at 22,421.95, down 718.55 points (3.10%) from last Friday's (25 Sep) 23,140.50, and Sensex at 71,909.70, down 1,986.04 points (2.69%) — a fourth straight red session for both indices, and September's entire F&O series closed as Nifty's worst monthly expiry in 25 years (-6.65%). The week's second big story was a single-stock one: IRDAI's insurance-distribution reform proposal crashed PB Fintech (Policybazaar) from ₹1,886.30 on 23 September to roughly ₹990 by Thursday — close to its IPO price — a ~47% wipeout in six sessions. Markets stayed shut all day today (Friday) for Gandhi Jayanti, but two fresh developments landed that bear on Monday's reopening: the grey market premium (GMP) on all three IPOs listing together on Monday moved — Orient Cables India to ~₹107 (~39%), German Green Steel & Power to ~₹12 (~9%), and AceVector (Snapdeal) stayed flat-to-muted (~₹0-2) — and Wall Street closed roughly flat Thursday night as the US 10-year yield eased off its high to ~5.24%. The next session is Monday, 5 October, when RBI's 3-day MPC meeting also begins and three IPOs list together.

Global Cues

Wall Street's week revolved entirely around US bond yields. As of Thursday night (1 Oct), the S&P 500 was up 0.19% at 7,666.45, the Nasdaq up 0.04% at 26,871.60, and the Dow up 0.04% at 50,926.56 — modest gains that clawed back morning losses as Treasury yields retreated slightly from their multi-decade highs. But the real story was the yield level itself: the US 10-year climbed to ~5.30% and the 30-year hit a 24-year high (~5.64%) — attributed to fiscal-deficit and Treasury-supply concerns, AI-driven corporate debt issuance, and reduced clarity on Fed forward guidance, rather than inflation fear. Wall Street closed roughly flat Thursday night (S&P +0.19%, Nasdaq +0.04%, Dow flat) as the yield eased slightly to ~5.24% — a small breather.

Brent crude's week also split in two: it spiked to ~$106-108 on Monday after the weekend's Trump-Iran Hormuz-reopening rejection, then eased gradually to ~$102-103 by Thursday-Friday as phased US-Iran talks continued in New York. The rupee stayed weak throughout, closing at ₹96.11/dollar on Thursday. Gold held near its recent highs, trading around ~$4,177-4,180/oz spot on Thursday.

Explainer

Thursday's most confusing data point was this: the odds of a Fed rate hike in October fell (from 51% to 38%), yet long-term US yields climbed anyway. Normally that's backwards — if the market expects a central bank to hold rates, long-dated bond yields should ease. The answer is the "term premium": extra compensation investors demand for holding long-dated bonds, separate from short-term policy-rate expectations. That premium rises when investors get nervous about a government's fiscal deficit and upcoming bond supply (US federal debt is now above $40 trillion), or when AI-boom-driven corporate debt issuance adds competition in the bond market. That's what happened this week — core inflation data was good, but fiscal and supply worries overshadowed it, and higher Treasury yields pulled FIIs out of Indian equities toward now-more-attractive, dollar-safe US bonds — a big part of why this week's FII outflow hit a record.

This Week's Concept
Position Sizing

Not every trade deserves the same amount of capital. Professional traders size a position based on how far their stop-loss is and how much of their total capital they're willing to risk on one idea — often just 1-2% per trade — rather than a fixed number of shares or lots. The strategy matters less than the size; a great setup with an oversized position can still wipe out an account. PB Fintech's ~47% crash in six sessions is exactly the kind of risk disciplined position sizing is built to manage — no single position should be large enough to cripple the whole portfolio.
