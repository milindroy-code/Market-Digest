Global Cues

Two big central-bank moves were the week's real story. The US Fed hiked 25 basis points Wednesday night, to 3.75%–4% — a unanimous vote, and the first hike since 2023 — but Chair Kevin Warsh's hawkish comments and a dot plot signalling at least one more hike this year (16 of 18 officials) sent the Dow down 631 points (1.21%) to 51,461.90. Wall Street staged a sharp rebound Thursday night — Dow +316 points (0.61%) to 51,778.04, S&P 500 +1.14% to 7,637.76, Nasdaq +1.69% to 26,418.30, led by tech (Intel +7%). Brent crude gave up Tuesday's $109.2 peak and eased through the week to ~$103–104/barrel by Friday — Saudi Arabia has flagged that roughly half the capacity of its damaged East-West pipeline should be restored within days, and full capacity within six weeks. The rupee stayed weak on dollar strength all week, sliding from ₹95.55 (Tue) to ₹96.03 (Thu) before firming slightly to close Friday at ₹95.76/$. Gold stayed elevated on safe-haven demand, up from ~$4,320 (Tue) to ~$4,385/oz (Fri) — roughly 1.5% for the week. The US 10-year Treasury yield climbed back above 5% after the Fed hike.

Explainer

All three confirmed sessions this week, FIIs were selling and DIIs were buying — in the same market, on the same days. Why does that keep happening? FIIs — global pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, foreign mutual funds — size their India allocation against global risk appetite: when US yields rise (as they did this week after the Fed hike), or the rupee weakens, the same Indian stock's dollar-denominated return looks less attractive to a foreign investor, even if nothing about the stock itself has changed. So they sell, for reasons that are global, not local. DIIs — domestic mutual funds, insurers, pension funds investing Indian savers' money — are largely insulated from that calculation: SIP inflows arrive every month regardless of whether the market is up or down, and they aren't benchmarking their returns against US Treasury yields. That's why, when foreign flows exit around a shock like a Fed hike or an oil spike, domestic flows usually hold steady or step up — this week, ₹10,212 crore of DII buying fully absorbed ₹8,219 crore of FII selling.

Strategy Corner
This Week's Concept
Calendar Spread

Sell a near-term option and buy a longer-dated option at the same strike. The near-term option decays faster (time decay accelerates as expiry approaches), so if the stock stays roughly flat, the position can profit from that difference in decay rates — a strategy built around time itself, not direction. A week like this one — a sharp early shock followed by a full week of consolidation back toward a range (Nifty ended the week almost exactly where it started) — is exactly the kind of setup where traders look at time-decay-based strategies instead of a clean directional bet.
