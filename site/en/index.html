Explainer

Why does a hot US CPI print rattle Indian markets? When US inflation comes in higher than expected, traders bet the Federal Reserve will need to keep interest rates elevated for longer, or hike again, to bring it under control. That pushes US government bond yields up, making dollar-denominated assets relatively more attractive — especially compared to emerging-market equities like India's, where returns carry extra currency risk. The result: Foreign Institutional Investors (FIIs) pull money out of EM stocks and into safer, higher-yielding US assets, putting selling pressure on indices like the Nifty and Sensex, even when India's own domestic story is perfectly fine. That's why Asia's chip-heavy markets — where foreign ownership runs high — fell so sharply this morning, and why GIFT Nifty is reflecting that mood too. A single data point rarely changes the whole cycle, but its hit to short-term sentiment is immediate.

Today's Concept
Max Pain Theory

Max Pain Theory holds that, by expiry, an underlying index or stock tends to gravitate toward the strike price at which option buyers, collectively, lose the most money — because option sellers (often larger, better-capitalized players) benefit from that outcome. It's a widely watched but imperfect signal, useful as one input among several rather than a guaranteed prediction. On a rough, uncertain pre-market day like today — with GIFT Nifty flat-to-negative and global cues mixed — traders tend to pay extra attention to positioning-based signals like this one, since price direction itself can be confusing to read. This is purely an educational concept, not a recommendation to place the trade.
