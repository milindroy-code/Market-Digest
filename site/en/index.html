Explainer: What A "Correction" Actually Means — And Why India Rarely Falls By The Same Scale

Overnight's biggest headline was that the "S&P 500 entered correction territory" — a specific technical term: a decline of 10% or more from a recent peak is called a "correction," while a decline of 20% or more is a "bear market." But that doesn't mean Indian investors should expect a matching 3-5% single-day crash today. Three reasons why: first, composition — the US selloff is heavily concentrated in AI/mega-cap tech (the Nasdaq's -5.3% against the Dow's comparatively milder -1.9% shows this divergence itself), while the Nifty is anchored in financials, FMCG and domestic-consumption names with far less direct AI-capex exposure. Second, flows — FIIs have been net buyers of Indian equities through most of November (~₹94,230cr over the first 13 sessions), a structural tailwind the US market doesn't have right now. Third, valuation reset — Nifty's own 2026 gain hasn't been as extreme as the US's AI-driven Nasdaq run, so there's less "air" to let out. None of this means India is immune — a sharp global repricing always pulls risk capital out of markets everywhere in the short term — but the size of the gap in how far each market falls is itself informative about what kind of story this is.

Today's Concept
Iron Condor

Sell an out-of-the-money call spread and an out-of-the-money put spread at the same time. You collect premium upfront and keep it if the underlying stays within a defined range through expiry — a trade that profits from the market going nowhere. Risk is capped on both sides, which is why it's a popular income strategy in sideways markets. Today's pick comes from the sequential rotation, tagged "range-bound" — an acknowledged mismatch, since today is expected to be the opposite of range-bound: after overnight's global crash, a sharp, directional gap-down open looks more likely than sideways consolidation. This is purely an educational concept, not a recommendation for any specific trade.
