Explainer: How Can An Index Fall When Most Stocks Are Rising?

Overnight, both the S&P 500 and Nasdaq fell — but 406 of the S&P's 500 stocks actually closed higher, and the equal-weight S&P index itself rose 1.2%. So why did the index fall? The answer is market-cap weighting — indices like the Nifty and S&P weight their member stocks by company size (market capitalization), not equally. This means a single multi-trillion-dollar company falling sharply can drag the whole index down, even if the other 495+ companies are rising. That's exactly what happened overnight — Alphabet alone falling 12.6% (on a market cap in the trillions) was enough to pull both the Nasdaq and S&P into the red, even though the market's actual "breadth" (how many stocks rose versus fell) was positive. For Indian investors, this is a useful habit: whenever you hear "the Nifty/Sensex fell," it's worth checking whether that's driven by one or two heavyweight stocks (like Reliance, HDFC Bank, or an IT major) or genuinely broad-based selling — the two mean very different things.

Strategy Corner
Today's Concept
Trailing Stop-Loss

Instead of a fixed stop-loss, a trailing stop moves up as the price rises, locking in gains while still giving the trade room to run. It's a way to let winners run — a piece of trading wisdom you'll see quoted often — without needing to predict exactly when a trend will end. After Thursday's strong +1.24%/+1.05% rebound, this is a fitting concept for traders who rode that move, especially with today's global cues mixed-to-soft and the Nifty possibly testing its new support. This is an educational concept, not a recommendation to place the trade.
