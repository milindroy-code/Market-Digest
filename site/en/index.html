Explainer: How An Open Offer Actually Works

When an acquirer like Tata Steel buys a 25%+ stake in a listed company, SEBI's Takeover Code (the SAST Regulations, 2011) requires it to make a "mandatory open offer" to the rest of the public shareholders too — for at least an additional 26% stake, at a fixed price. The process follows defined steps: first a public announcement, then a "draft letter of offer" is filed with SEBI; after SEBI's comments, an "identified date" (similar to a record date) is fixed, determining who is eligible; a formal "letter of offer" is then sent to eligible shareholders; and a roughly 10-working-day "tendering window" opens, during which shareholders can choose to sell their shares at the offer price (or not — it's optional). The whole process typically takes several weeks from announcement to payment. For the Tata Steel-APL Apollo deal, this formal timeline — the record date, the letter-of-offer date — had not been announced anywhere in this run; this digest will update as filings come in.

Bull Call Spread

In a Bull Call Spread, you buy a lower-strike call and simultaneously sell a higher-strike call, same expiry — this caps both your cost and your maximum profit compared with buying a call outright, making it a cheaper way to express a moderately bullish view, in exchange for giving up unlimited upside for a lower breakeven. Today's pick from the sequential rotation, tagged "uptrend" — there's no trading session today, but the concept offers a forward-looking frame: if Wednesday brings a recovery bounce from Monday's selloff, a Bull Call Spread is one way to capture that move cheaply, without unlimited-upside exposure. This is purely an educational concept, not a recommendation for any specific trade, nor a prediction of Wednesday's move.
