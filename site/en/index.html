Macro & Policy

Overnight, the US Trade Representative announced its recommendations from a "structural excess capacity" investigation covering 16 economies — India received a proposed tariff of 25% without a deal, 17% with a trade agreement, the second-highest base rate on the list after Vietnam's 35%. USTR chief Jamieson Greer said the tariffs would act as a "top-up" (not stacked on existing duties), and that they're unlikely to take effect for at least "a couple of months" — written submissions are due by 5 January, with a public hearing on 25 January. India's commerce ministry has rejected the excess-capacity allegation, calling the notice "under examination" and pointing to the ongoing bilateral trade-agreement talks as the right forum to resolve it. This is distinct from February 2026's interim deal, which addressed IEEPA-based "reciprocal tariffs" (later struck down by the US Supreme Court) — this new proposal comes via a different legal route, a Section 301 "excess capacity" investigation (full mechanism in today's Explainer, below). Explainer: What A Section 301 "Excess Capacity" Tariff Actually Is — And Why It's Different From The Deal We Already Have

This morning's big story — a new 25% US tariff proposal on India — can be confusing, since we already covered an India-US trade deal in February 2026 that cut tariffs to 18%. That deal addressed "reciprocal tariffs" under IEEPA, a mechanism the US Supreme Court later ruled unlawful — leaving that entire framework in limbo while both sides negotiate a new interim agreement. Today's proposal comes from an entirely different legal tool: Section 301 of the Trade Act, which lets the USTR investigate another country's "unfair trade practices" and respond with tariffs — here, via a probe into 16 economies' "structural excess manufacturing capacity" (producing more than domestic demand and exporting the surplus globally). For India, the USTR specifically flagged US "dependence" on Indian pharma, steel and ICT goods. The two tools are unrelated, but the result is the same for investors: fresh uncertainty until the final rate (25% or 17%) and timeline are settled — which is months away, so for now this is headline risk, not an actual tariff.

Today's Concept
Averaging Down vs. Pyramiding

Averaging down means buying more of a losing position to lower your average cost — risky, since you're adding to a trade that's already going against you. Pyramiding is the opposite: adding to a winning position as it moves in your favor. Most experienced traders favor pyramiding into strength over averaging into weakness, since it means the market is confirming the thesis rather than you fighting it. Today's pick comes from the sequential rotation, tagged "concept" — and today is a good day for the distinction: GIFT Nifty is signalling a second straight gap-up (which could tempt traders to pyramid into an already-rallying position), but overnight's tariff-proposal headline has also brought a fresh, still-unresolved risk. For traders already in yesterday's rally, this is a good moment to ask whether they're adding to strength or simply chasing a move. This is purely an educational concept, not a recommendation for any specific trade.
