Explainer: Negative Free Cash Flow — When a Company Spends More Than It Earns

Free cash flow (FCF) is what's left over after a company pays for its operations and then invests in capital expenditure — new assets, factories, data centers. When that number turns negative, it means the company is spending more on capex than it's earning from operations, and that gap has to be funded with debt or cash reserves. Overnight, Meta reported its first negative FCF in over a decade, driven directly by its massive AI data-center and chip-buying spend. Investors take this seriously because it's a direct signal that AI-infrastructure build-out has become so expensive that even the most profitable tech companies can no longer fund it from operating cash alone — they have to borrow or draw down reserves. If that trend continues, it raises the question of whether that spending will actually generate matching revenue growth, which is the core of the entire "AI trade" valuation thesis. That's the doubt that triggered a chain reaction through the Nasdaq, the SOX (the chip-makers' index), and then Asia — one US company's balance-sheet disclosure rattling chip-adjacent stocks worldwide.

Today's Concept
Risk-Reward Ratio

Before entering any trade, compare what you stand to lose (the distance to your stop-loss) against what you stand to gain (the distance to your target). A trade with a 1:3 risk-reward ratio can still be profitable overall even if you're wrong more often than you're right — which is why professional traders talk about expectancy, not just win rate. A day like today, with a divergent signal — GIFT Nifty's gap-up against overnight global weakness — only raises the value of discipline and a risk-reward framework decided in advance. This is an educational concept, not a recommendation for any specific trade.
