Explainer: How A Hormuz Scare Hits Markets Through The Rupee And Inflation

India imports about 85% of its crude requirement, and historically 40-50% of that passed through the Strait of Hormuz (one government statement says about 70% of imports are now routed around it, up from roughly 55% earlier). Whenever reports of tanker attacks in Hormuz surface, crude jumps immediately — but the real hit doesn't land on oil companies directly; it travels through a chain reaction: costlier crude → a bigger import bill → higher dollar demand → a weaker rupee (₹92.98/$ today) → fears of imported inflation. A 10% rise in crude prices can lift India's CPI inflation by roughly 30 basis points, per one analysis citing RBI data. That's why autos and other rate-sensitive, consumption-facing sectors tend to feel it most on days like this — while today's IT selloff, notably, had nothing to do with crude; it was a separate profit-booking story.

Today's Concept
Max Pain Theory

The idea that an underlying tends to gravitate toward the strike price at which option buyers, collectively, lose the most money by expiry — because option sellers (often larger, better-capitalized players) benefit from that outcome. It's a widely watched but imperfect signal, useful as one input rather than a guaranteed prediction. Today's pick comes from the sequential rotation, tagged "concept" — and it connects directly to the gap flagged in today's Technicals section (no fresh PCR/max-pain reading available): when fresh options-positioning data is missing, concepts like max pain are a reminder that the options market has its own gravitational pull, one that can move independently of the headline index swings. This is purely an educational concept, not a recommendation for any specific trade.
